How Arandu performs with real AI providers

The AdapterBenchmark sample sent the same ten tasks, five times each, to three real language models: Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite through the Gemini adapter, and GPT-OSS 120B and GPT-OSS 20B through the Groq adapter. This report explains what was measured, what the numbers show about the adapters and about the plugin's own features, and what to improve next.

00 Key findings

Five things this run shows

The short version. Each finding links to the section with the evidence.

1

Arandu adds very little time

On turns without retries, the plugin's own work (Core, adapter, Unity frame wait) took a median of 7–14 ms. That is about 1–2% of a turn. Almost all the time is spent on the network and at the provider.

 2

Groq's rate limits dominate its results

Groq answers fast, but its rate limit (tokens per minute) made the plugin wait. Waiting for rate limits was about 91% of all Groq turn time. Without those waits, both Groq models were faster than Gemini in nearly every scenario.

 3

The retry logic behaved correctly

The plugin retried 90 times and always waited at least as long as the provider asked (Retry-After) in 81 of 82 retried turns. The one exception ended on purpose: the requested wait did not fit in the turn's 60 s deadline.

 4

Two adapter-level problems found

The Gemini adapter rejects the StructuredOutput schema before sending it (0 of 5), because the schema uses maxLength. GPT-OSS 20B once sent a tool call the Groq adapter could not read. Both are concrete fixes.

 5

Context summaries never got accepted

Context pressure was detected in all 15 long conversations and the history stayed bounded, so the scenario passed. But the 30 summary attempts produced no accepted summary: Gemini's were cut off and Groq's hit the rate limit.

01 The benchmark

What was run, and on what

A quick refresher on the words used in the rest of the report, then the exact setup.

Adapter

The piece of Arandu that talks to one AI provider. It turns an Arandu request into the provider's HTTP format and turns the answer back. This run used the Gemini and Groq example adapters.

Model

The language model behind the provider. Groq hosts two open-weight models here (GPT-OSS 120B and 20B, the number is their size in billions of parameters). Gemini hosts Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite.

Scenario

One fixed task that tests one plugin feature, such as calling a tool or remembering earlier messages. There are ten.

Turn

One request from the game and its final answer. A scenario can have several turns (a conversation). The 10 scenarios add up to 19 turns.

Repeat

Each scenario ran 5 times per model, each time in a fresh session, to see how consistent the results are.

Expectation

A concrete pass/fail check per turn, written in the sample. For example, "used the lookup tool and the answer contains 7". It checks behavior, not writing quality.

Retry

When a provider call fails with a temporary error, Arandu can wait and send it again. The wait follows the provider's Retry-After header when present, plus backoff.

Rate limit

A cap the provider puts on usage, like requests or tokens per minute. Going over it returns HTTP 429 ("too many requests") with a time to wait.

02 Reading the numbers

How the results are summarized

The analysis follows common practice for latency benchmarks and for evaluating AI agents. With 5 repeats per scenario, the sample is small, so the report favors simple, robust measures and always shows the raw points.

Median and p90, not the average

Response times are skewed: most calls are quick, a few are very slow. One slow call can pull the average far from what is typical. The median (p50) is the middle value. The p90 is the time that 90% of turns stayed under, a view of the slow tail. Higher percentiles like p99 need hundreds of samples, so they are not used here.

Pass rate with an honest margin

A pass rate from 5 tries is uncertain. Each rate comes with a 95% Wilson interval, a range that stays sensible for small samples and rates near 0% or 100%. Each scenario also reports pass^5: how many of the 5 repeats passed on every turn. It rewards consistency, an idea from the τ-bench agent benchmark.

Waiting is not slowness

When a provider is rate-limited, Arandu waits on purpose. That time says nothing about how fast the model is. So each turn's time is split into four parts (below), and speed is shown with and without the rate-limit wait. Model comparisons use Cliff's delta, a simple effect size: how often one model is faster than the other, from −1 to +1.

The four parts of a turn

Arandu & UnityEverything done on the PC: Core phases (queue, preparation, context, checkpoint), the adapter's encoding and decoding, tools, and waiting for the next Unity frame.
Network & provider front endHTTP time that the provider does not report as processing: the trip over the internet, TLS, the provider's queue and gateway.
Provider processingThe processing time the provider states in its response (Gemini's Server-Timing header, Groq's usage.total_time).
Rate-limit waitTime Arandu waited before a retry, as asked by the provider. Zero when no retry was needed.
Data cleaning

Unity was minimized a few times during the Gemini run. A minimized Editor slows its frame loop to about 10 frames per second and can pause the benchmark between turns. Four Gemini turns ran during those moments: their Unity frame wait was about 100 ms instead of about 4 ms. These four turns (SkillSwitch repeat 4 turn 2, ContextPressure repeat 1 turn 5, and repeat 3 turns 6 and 7) are left out of every timing chart and statistic. Their pass/fail results are kept, because they all completed correctly. The pauses between turns (31.5 minutes) are removed from the session clock.

The diagnostic build measures these parts directly (commit 6ac5285). Provider processing is the provider's own statement, not a local measurement, and the two providers report it differently, so compare it with care.

Outside the two adapter issues, almost everything passed: tools, mutations with receipts, multi-turn memory, skills, cancellation and long inputs. The failures fall into three groups. Which model is faster? Effect size per scenario

Cliff's delta on turn time without rate-limit waits, completed turns only. A value of +1.00 means every turn of the first model was slower than every turn of the second. Labels follow the usual thresholds (Romano et al., 2006): negligible under 0.15, small under 0.33, medium under 0.47, large above. With 5–35 turns per cell this shows direction and size, not statistical proof.

What this means

Measured on total time, Gemini looks fastest in the heavy scenarios, but only because it was rarely rate-limited. Remove the waits and the picture flips: GPT-OSS 20B is the quickest, GPT-OSS 120B is next, and Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite is the slowest of the three in almost every scenario. For a game, both views matter: raw speed tells what the model can do, total time tells what a player on this provider plan would feel.

What this means

The plugin is not the bottleneck. A typical turn spends single-digit milliseconds in Arandu: about 0–2 ms in the Core phases, 2–3 ms waiting for the next Unity frame to resume, under 2 ms in the adapter, and about 4 ms handing the request in and the result back to the game. The slowest local times (up to 87 ms) were the first calls of the session, while code and connections warm up. Gemini's median is higher because of its last turns: before the long pauses its median was 9 ms, close to Groq's; in the 28 clean turns after Unity was restored it was 37 ms, with the hand-back alone taking about 18 ms instead of 4 ms. That points to the Editor still running at a lower frame rate (for example, while not in focus), not to extra work in Arandu. For Gemini, the provider's own processing is most of the time. For Groq, processing is tiny (about 0.1 s), and the rest is network, the provider's queue and, above all, rate-limit waits.

06 Rate limits and retries

What happened when providers said "slow down"

Groq's rate-limit documentation lists 8,000 tokens per minute and 30 requests per minute for both GPT-OSS models in its standard limits table (the limits of this account were not checked). The benchmark sends long inputs every 2.5 s, so it reaches the token cap quickly. This is where Arandu's retry feature was really exercised.

The whole session, turn by turn

Each dot is a turn, placed at its start time · ring = the turn had to wait for a rate limit

The clock skips the eight pauses while Unity was minimized (dotted lines, 31.5 minutes in total; the longest was about 12 minutes). The four hollow gray dots ran while the Editor was minimized and are left out of the timing charts. On the Groq side, the turns marked with a ring cluster in the long ContextPressure and LongInput scenarios, where each request carries thousands of tokens.

Bigger requests, longer waits

Groq turns · request size vs. rate-limit wait

Each dot is one Groq turn. Under 1,000 tokens, only 12 of 93 turns waited (at most 4.6 s). At 3,000 tokens or more, 44 of 50 turns waited, with a median of 28 s, because a token-per-minute limit refills slowly. Groq's documentation also says cached prompt tokens do not count toward the limit, so the exact budget per request cannot be rebuilt from these files.

Did Arandu wait long enough?

Each dot is a retried turn · wait asked vs. wait done, seconds

Dots on or above the diagonal waited at least as long as the provider asked. 81 of 82 are there. The single dot far below is the Gemini ToolChain run whose second Retry-After (58 s) did not fit in the 60 s turn deadline, so the run ended as ModelUnavailable instead of waiting.

What this means

Retries rescued 80 of the 82 turns that needed them (79 of 80 on Groq), which is the goal of the feature. The cost is time: in ContextPressure, the later turns waited up to 44 s each. Two ideas follow. First, the benchmark's Groq totals describe this account's rate limits more than the models. Second, a game on such a plan would benefit from pacing by tokens before sending (Groq returns x-ratelimit-remaining-tokens on every response), instead of only reacting to 429 errors.

07 Plugin features under test

Feature by feature

Beyond speed, each scenario checks one Arandu capability with a live model. Here is what each one showed.

Tools

Read tools and tool chains

The model may only propose a tool call. Arandu runs the tool and sends the result back, so one turn needs several model calls. All models used the lookup tool correctly in all 15 SingleReadTool turns. In ToolChain (three lookups, then a sum), 12 of 15 turns passed; the three failures were two rate-limit give-ups and one unreadable tool call.

Model calls per turn, median. Gemini can request several tools at once, so it needs fewer calls. Failed calls that were retried count too.

Mutations

Changing game state safely

In MutationTool the model asks to add 3 to a counter. The game's authority applies it and issues a receipt, and Arandu then re-checks the operation. 15 of 15 turns reported the change as Applied and the re-check as Resolved.

0applied, with receipt
0re-checks resolved
0duplicate effects

The counter went 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 across repeats, so the sample's counter lives for the whole session rather than per repeat. Even when the follow-up model call was retried, the mutation was not applied twice.

Context

Context pressure in long conversations

ContextPressure sends seven large messages in one session. Arandu should notice the history is getting too big and keep it within budget. It did: pressure was detected at turn 6 in all 15 runs and the history stopped growing at 12 messages (about 28.5 KB).

Average over the 5 repeats. The marker shows where pressure was detected.

Summaries

Context summaries

When pressure is detected, Arandu can ask the model to summarize older messages. It tried 30 times (turns 6 and 7 of each run) and accepted none. The turns still worked because the history was bounded, but the summary path itself did not succeed with live providers.

Gemini returned summaries cut off by the output limit (Truncated) or rate-limited. Groq's summary calls were rate-limited; one GPT-OSS 20B summary completed but was not accepted.

Skills

Switching skills mid-session

SkillSwitch asks for a sunset description with a terse skill ("one short sentence"), then again with a detailed skill ("three to five sentences"). All 30 turns completed. The length change shows whether the model followed the new skill.

Sessions

Memory across turns

MultiTurnSession gives a code word, asks an unrelated question, then asks for the code word back. Recall depends on Arandu sending the session history correctly.

Every model, every repeat. This is the plain session-history path working end to end.

Cancellation

Stopping a request

Cancellation asks for a 400-word essay and cancels after 800 ms. Arandu should stop the run quickly and report Cancelled. It did in 14 of 15 turns. The chart shows the time from the cancel request to the final status.

Groq runs stopped within 5–8 ms, Gemini runs within 21–41 ms. The one miss was a quick refusal that finished before the cancel.

Structured output

Answers in a JSON schema

StructuredOutput asks for {answer, confidence} following a JSON schema. Both Groq models returned valid JSON in 10 of 10 turns, for example {"answer":"Paris","confidence":100}.

The Gemini adapter stopped all 5 turns in about 1 ms, before any network call, with UnsupportedCapability. The schema contains "maxLength": 200, and the adapter deliberately rejects keywords it does not translate (GeminiProtocol.StripDialect). Gemini's documentation says unsupported schema fields may be ignored, so the adapter chose to fail closed. This is safe, but it means a common schema keyword blocks the feature.

09 Conclusions

What the run tells us, and what to do next

Gemini 3.5 Flash LiteGPT-OSS 120B (Groq)GPT-OSS 20B (Groq)

For the adapters

  • Gemini schema keywords. Translate or drop maxLength and minLength (with a documented rule), or report which keyword caused UnsupportedCapability, so StructuredOutput can work on Gemini.
  • Groq tool calls. Look at the one InvalidResponse from GPT-OSS 20B. ThroneRoom already retries Groq's tool_use_failed; the benchmark connector could do the same.
  • Proactive pacing. Read Groq's x-ratelimit-remaining-tokens to wait before a call instead of after a 429.

For the plugin

  • Summaries. Find out why no summary was accepted: Gemini's were truncated, so the summary output budget may be too small; Groq's hit the rate limit, so summaries could use the same retry path.
  • Request estimate. Arandu's estimated request size was about 4–6× the input tokens the provider measured (median per model). It is a safe upper bound, but a closer estimate would delay pressure handling until it is needed.
  • Overhead. No action needed: 1–2% of a turn. Keep the diagnostic timing to catch regressions.

For the next benchmark run

  • Use a plan with higher rate limits, or a longer delay for Groq, to measure the model rather than the quota; or keep the current limits on purpose and report them.
  • Interleave models instead of running them in blocks, so time-of-day effects do not favor one model.
  • Add more repeats for the cheap scenarios and test a third provider, as planned.
  • Reset the mutation counter per repeat, and make the cancellation prompt long enough that it cannot finish in 800 ms.

Limits of this report

  • One session, one PC, one network, in the Unity Editor. No player build or device was measured.
  • 5 repeats per scenario: good for spotting problems, too few for fine-grained comparisons.
  • Expectations check behavior (status, tool use, a number or word in the answer), not answer quality.
  • Provider processing times are the providers' statements. Cost was not available from either provider.
  • No fault injection: retries appeared only because the live providers triggered them.
